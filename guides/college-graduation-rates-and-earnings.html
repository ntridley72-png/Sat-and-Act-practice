College Graduation Rates, Retention and Earnings: What They Tell You

Graduation rates, first-year retention and earnings are three of the most useful numbers on a college profile, but each one measures something narrower than it sounds. This guide explains what the U.S. Department of Education's College Scorecard actually counts, and how to use the numbers without over-reading them.

Graduation rate

The Scorecard graduation rate is the share of first-time, full-time, degree-seeking students who finished within 150% of the expected time (six years for a four-year degree). It does not count part-time students or students who transferred in, so it can understate completion at colleges with many of those students.

First-year retention

Retention is the share of first-time, full-time students who return for a second year. It is an early signal of whether students are supported and satisfied, and a low number can predict a lower graduation rate.

Earnings after college

Median earnings are measured for students who received federal aid, were working and were not enrolled, ten years after they entered. The figure is adjusted for inflation. It reflects who attends (major mix, region and aid status) as much as what the college adds, so compare similar colleges and majors.

Debt and aid

  • Median federal debt is the typical loan principal when borrowers enter repayment.
  • Federal loan recipients is the share of undergraduates borrowing federal loans.
  • Pell Grant recipients shows the share of students from lower-income families, a sign of how the college serves them.

How to use these numbers

  • Compare colleges of a similar type and selectivity, not a research university against a small college.
  • Pair outcomes with net price. See what college really costs.
  • Look at several measures together: a high graduation rate with high retention and moderate debt is a stronger sign than any one of them.
  • Remember the periods. Scorecard measures come from older student cohorts and can lag a college's current performance.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good college graduation rate?

Six-year graduation rates vary widely. Compare a college with others of similar selectivity and type, since open-admission colleges and highly selective ones serve very different students.

Why is a college's graduation rate low?

It may serve many part-time, transfer or working students, who are not counted in the six-year figure, or it may not support students well. Check retention, advising and aid as well.

What does first-year retention mean?

It is the percentage of first-time, full-time students who return for their second year. Higher is better, and it hints at how supported students feel.

Are the earnings numbers what I will make?

No. They are median earnings for federal-aid recipients ten years after entering, and they depend heavily on major and region. Use them to compare colleges, not to predict your pay.

Where does this data come from?

From the U.S. Department of Education's College Scorecard, using IPEDS, NSLDS and Treasury data. Each profile on FunSAT lists the reporting period and source.

Do graduation rates include transfer students?

No. The Scorecard graduation rate covers first-time, full-time students who started at that college, so transfers are not counted.
