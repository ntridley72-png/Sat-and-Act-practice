How Much Does College Really Cost? Sticker Price vs Net Price

The tuition number on a college website is rarely what a family pays. The figure that matters is the net price: what you pay after grants and scholarships. This guide explains the difference, what goes into a year of costs, and how to compare colleges fairly.

Sticker price vs net price

Sticker price is the published cost: tuition, fees and often room and board. Net price subtracts grant and scholarship aid. In the U.S. Department of Education's College Scorecard, average net price is for first-time, full-time students who receive federal aid, and it is reported by family-income band at many colleges. A private college with a high sticker price can cost less than a public one after aid, which is why comparing net prices is more useful than comparing tuition.

What a year of college includes

  • Tuition and fees, which differ for in-state and out-of-state students at public colleges.
  • Room and board on or off campus.
  • Books and supplies, plus a laptop if you need one.
  • Transportation home and around campus.
  • Personal expenses such as phone, health costs and spending money.

Colleges publish a cost of attendance that adds these up. Check it, then check the net price for your family's income.

In-state vs out-of-state

At most public universities, out-of-state tuition is much higher than in-state tuition. Some states and regional programs let neighboring students pay close to in-state rates, and a few colleges give strong merit aid to out-of-state students. Compare your own state's public colleges first, using each state's cost page.

How to compare colleges by cost

  1. Use each college's net price calculator. Every college that takes federal aid must have one on its website.
  2. Compare net price by income band on each college's profile, not the average, since aid depends on your family's income.
  3. Add four years of cost, and ask whether the aid is guaranteed to continue.
  4. Look at median federal debt and graduation rates. Paying a lower price at a school you are unlikely to finish is not a saving. See graduation rates and earnings.

Ways to lower the bill

  • File the FAFSA every year. It unlocks federal aid and most state and college aid.
  • Apply for scholarships, starting with local and small awards where the pool is smaller. See how to find and win scholarships.
  • Check test-score thresholds for merit aid. A higher SAT or ACT score can lower your price at some colleges.
  • Consider starting at a community college, living at home, or choosing colleges that meet full demonstrated need.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between sticker price and net price?

Sticker price is the published cost before aid. Net price is what a student pays after grants and scholarships. Net price is the number to compare between colleges.

Is the net price in College Scorecard what I will pay?

It is an average for first-time, full-time students who receive federal aid, so your own price can differ. Use the college's net price calculator and compare the income band closest to your family's.

How much does a year of college cost?

It depends on the college and your aid. Look at the cost of attendance, which includes tuition, fees, housing, food, books and personal costs, and then subtract the grant aid you expect.

Is a private college always more expensive than a public one?

No. Private colleges have higher sticker prices but often give more grant aid, so the net price for some families is lower than at a public college, especially out of state.

Can test scores lower the cost of college?

At many colleges, yes. Merit scholarships often have SAT or ACT thresholds. Check each college's scholarship page for score requirements.

Where can I see college costs by state?

Our college-costs pages show average costs for every state, and each college profile shows its net price and the net price by family income where the college reports it.
