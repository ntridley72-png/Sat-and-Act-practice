College list guide

How to Build a College List: Reach, Match and Likely Schools

A good college list has a few schools you would love, several you are likely to get into, and at least one you are almost sure to get into and can afford. This guide shows how to sort colleges into reach, match and likely using real score ranges, then narrow the list by cost and fit.

Reach, match and likely

  • Reach: your scores and grades are below the college's typical range, or the college admits a small share of applicants (even strong applicants can be turned away).
  • Match: your scores sit inside the middle 50% range and the acceptance rate is moderate.
  • Likely: your scores are at or above the 75th percentile and the college admits most applicants. Some people call these "safety" schools; here "safety" is about admission, not campus safety. For campus safety, see how to check a campus.

How to sort a college using its numbers

  1. Find the college's middle 50% SAT or ACT range. A quarter of enrolled students scored below the low number and a quarter above the high one.
  2. Compare your score. Above the 75th percentile is a strength, inside the range is neutral, below the 25th percentile means the rest of your application must do more work.
  3. Check the acceptance rate. Under about 25% is a reach for nearly everyone, whatever the score.
  4. Check whether the college is test-optional. See test-optional colleges explained.

Beyond the numbers: fit, cost and safety

  • Cost: compare net price, not sticker price. See what college really costs.
  • Outcomes: graduation rate, retention and earnings, as explained in our outcomes guide.
  • Size and setting: enrollment, student-to-faculty ratio and city or rural setting.
  • Safety and support: read the campus-safety report and visit if you can.
  • Majors: confirm the programs you want exist and are not capped.

A step-by-step plan

  1. Take a practice test and get an unofficial score estimate.
  2. List 15 to 20 colleges across a range of selectivity. Start with our college matcher, which sorts colleges into reach, match and likely from your score, budget and preferences.
  3. Sort them into reach, match and likely.
  4. Remove any you cannot afford after aid.
  5. Trim to about 8 to 12 colleges: two or three reaches, four or five matches, and two or three likely schools.
  6. Compare the finalists side by side in the comparison tool.

Frequently asked questions

How many colleges should I apply to?

Most students apply to about 8 to 12: two or three reaches, four or five matches and two or three likely schools. More applications cost money and time without improving your odds much.

What is a match school?

A match school is one where your SAT or ACT score sits inside the college's middle 50% range and the acceptance rate is moderate, so admission is realistic but not certain.

What is a safety school?

A safety, or likely, school is one where your scores are at or above the 75th percentile and the college admits most applicants. Pick one you would happily attend and can afford.

Does a low acceptance rate always make a college a reach?

Usually. A college that admits fewer than about one in four applicants is a reach for most students, even with scores in range, because many strong applicants are turned away.

Should test scores decide my college list?

They are one input. Grades, courses, essays, cost and fit matter too, and some colleges are test-optional. Use scores to sort colleges, then choose by fit and cost.
