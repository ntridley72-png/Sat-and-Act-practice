Is This College Campus Safe? How to Check Before You Apply

Parents and students search "is this college safe" more than almost any other college question, and the honest answer is that no single number settles it. What you can do is read the federal campus-crime reports the right way, then add what you learn from a visit. This guide shows where the official numbers live, what they mean, and how to avoid the common traps.

What the Clery Act requires

Colleges that take federal financial aid must report crime on and around their campuses under the Clery Act (20 U.S.C. 1092(f)). Each school publishes an Annual Security Report every year, keeps a public crime log if it has a campus police or security department, and must issue timely warnings about ongoing threats. The numbers are reported in groups:

  • Criminal offenses: for example murder, rape, robbery, aggravated assault, burglary and motor-vehicle theft.
  • VAWA offenses: domestic violence, dating violence and stalking.
  • Hate crimes reported in the same categories.
  • Arrests and disciplinary referrals for weapons, drug and liquor-law violations.

Counts are split by geography: on campus, on-campus student housing (a subset of on-campus, never added to it), noncampus property the school owns or controls, and public property next to campus.

Where to find a college's safety numbers

  1. The U.S. Department of Education's Campus Safety and Security tool at ope.ed.gov/campussafety lets you look up any college and compare three years of reported counts.
  2. The college's own Annual Security Report. Search "[college name] annual security and fire safety report". It also lists emergency contacts, escort services, reporting options and housing fire safety.
  3. The campus daily crime log kept by the campus police, which shows recent incidents.
  4. A campus visit to see lighting, emergency phones, transit at night and how students talk about safety.

How to read the numbers without being misled

  • Reported is not the same as total. The figures are incidents that were reported within defined boundaries. They do not measure how much crime actually happens.
  • A higher count can mean better reporting. Campuses that make it easy and safe to report, and that encourage reporting, can look worse on paper than campuses where students stay silent.
  • Compare per student, not raw totals. A school with 40,000 students will report more incidents than one with 1,500. Divide by enrollment, and look at the same offense across several schools.
  • Look at the trend. One year can jump or drop for a single reason. Three years show the pattern.
  • Check the geography. Housing counts are part of on-campus counts. Do not add them together, and remember that noncampus and public-property counts cover different places.
  • A blank is not a zero. Only a printed 0 means the school reported none.
  • The city matters. A campus in a large city shares streets with everyone else. Look at the surrounding neighborhood and the school's transportation and alert systems too.

A campus-safety checklist for visits

  • Is there a 24-hour campus police or security presence, and how do you reach them?
  • Does the college send emergency text and email alerts? Ask to see an example.
  • Are there late-night shuttles, escort services or safe-ride programs?
  • How are residence halls secured, and who can enter?
  • Who is the Title IX coordinator, and where do students report harassment or assault?
  • What mental-health, counseling and health services are on campus, and how long is the wait?
  • How does the school handle alcohol and drug policies, and what support exists for students who need it?

Frequently asked questions

Is campus crime data reliable?

It is the best official source, but it is limited. The figures are incidents reported within the Clery Act's geography, as filed by each college. They do not measure true prevalence, and reporting practices differ, so treat them as one input rather than a ranking.

Where can I see crime statistics for any college?

The U.S. Department of Education's Campus Safety and Security tool at ope.ed.gov/campussafety lets you search any college. Each college also publishes an Annual Security Report on its website.

Does a higher number of reported crimes mean a college is less safe?

Not necessarily. A campus that encourages reporting and makes it easy can show higher counts than one where students under-report. Compare per-student rates, look at three years of data and combine it with what you see on a visit.

Do campus crime numbers include student housing?

Yes. On-campus counts include student-housing incidents, and housing is also published as its own table. Do not add the two together, or you count housing incidents twice.

What is an Annual Security Report?

It is a report each college must publish by October 1 each year under the Clery Act. It contains three years of crime statistics, campus security policies, reporting procedures and fire-safety information.

How do I check if my college has a Title IX office?

Search the college's website for Title IX coordinator. Every federally funded college must have one, and the Annual Security Report lists how to report sexual harassment and assault.
