SAT Score Calculator: How Raw Scores Become a 1600

Every SAT score you have ever seen started as a count of correct answers and then went through two transformations. Understanding them tells you how many questions you can actually afford to miss, and why your friend's "score calculator" disagrees with yours by twenty points.

Step 1: raw score

Your raw score is simply the number of questions you answered correctly. One point each, every question weighted the same at this stage, and — critically — no penalty for a wrong answer. The old quarter-point deduction for guessing was removed years ago and has not come back.

The practical consequence: never leave a question blank. A blank and a wrong answer cost exactly the same, and a blind guess among four options is worth a quarter of a point in expectation. On a section where you are running out of time, spending the last fifteen seconds filling in every remaining bubble is free expected points.

Step 2: scaled score

Each section's raw score is converted to a scaled score between 200 and 800 using a conversion table built for that specific test form. The two scaled section scores — Reading & Writing, and Math — add together for a total between 400 and 1600.

This conversion is what people loosely call "the curve", and it is the reason scores are comparable across test dates. It is not a curve in the classroom sense: it is not affected by how other students did on your test date. It is set in advance through equating, so that a given scaled score means the same ability regardless of which form you sat.

Notice how the curve steepens at the top. Between raw 44 and raw 40 you lose 60 points for four questions — 15 points each. Between raw 32 and raw 28 you lose 40 points for four questions — 10 points each. Errors near the top of the scale cost roughly fifty percent more than errors in the middle. This is the mathematical reason why high scorers should prioritise eliminating careless mistakes over attempting the hardest questions.

Adaptive scoring: why correct-answer counts no longer tell the whole story

The digital SAT is section-adaptive. Each section has two modules. Everyone gets the same first module; your performance on it determines whether your second module is the easier or the harder version.

Scoring then accounts for which module you received. A student who takes the harder second module and misses six questions can outscore a student who takes the easier one and misses three, because the questions were not worth the same. Two implications:

  • Raw-count calculators are approximations on the adaptive format in a way they were not on paper. Treat any number they give you as a range, not a point.
  • Module one accuracy is leveraged. It does not just earn its own points, it unlocks the higher-scoring module. Rushing it to save time is a bad trade. See how to improve your SAT score.

Superscoring

Many colleges superscore: they take your best Reading & Writing from any sitting and your best Math from any sitting and combine them. A student who scores 700/650 and then 660/710 has a 1350 and a 1370 on paper, but a 1410 superscore. Check each college's policy — it is usually on the admissions site under testing — because where superscoring applies, a retake carries almost no downside.

Frequently asked questions

How is the SAT scored?

One raw point per correct answer, no penalty for wrong ones. Each section's raw score converts to 200–800 on a curve specific to that test form, and the two add to a 400–1600 total.

Is there a penalty for guessing?

No. Answer every question, including blind guesses.

How many questions can you miss for a 1500?

Roughly four to eight across the whole test, depending on the curve and how the misses split between sections.

Why do SAT score calculators disagree?

Each test form has its own conversion table, so calculators built on different published practice tests differ by 10 to 30 points. Any raw-to-scaled estimate is an approximation unless it uses your exact form.

How does adaptive scoring affect my score?

Questions are weighted by difficulty and your second module's difficulty depends on your first module, so two students with identical correct-answer counts can receive different scaled scores.
